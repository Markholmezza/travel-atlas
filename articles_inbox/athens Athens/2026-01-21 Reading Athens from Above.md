From the rocks around the Acropolis, Athens looks like a single white surface poured across the basin all the way to the mountains. Up close, it turns out to be a catalogue of two centuries of building, mostly put up one plot at a time.

## Neoclassical beginnings

When Athens became the capital of the new Greek state in 1834, it was a small town. The architects of the young kingdom planned a European capital in a neoclassical style that looked back to the monuments on the hill: columns, pediments, marble balconies and painted plaster. The grandest examples are the Academy, the University and the National Library on Panepistimiou Street; smaller townhouses survive in Plaka and Thiseio, many restored as shops and offices.

## The polykatoikia

Most of what fills the view, though, is the *polykatoikia*: the reinforced-concrete apartment block of six or seven storeys with a deep balcony on every floor. After the Second World War, landowners often handed a plot to a builder in exchange for a share of the flats built on it, an arrangement known as *antiparochi*. It needed little capital, so the city grew quickly and densely, block by block, without large developers.

The balconies are the most practical part of the design. Awnings roll down in summer, plants spill over the railings, and on warm evenings each balcony becomes an extra room facing the street.

## Ancient and modern side by side

The Temple of Hephaestus, one of the best-preserved Greek temples anywhere, stands on its low hill above the Ancient Agora with apartment blocks rising behind it. Seen from above, the contrast is the point: a city that kept building around its ruins rather than clearing space for them.
