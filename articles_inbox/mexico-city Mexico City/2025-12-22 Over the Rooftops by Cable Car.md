Some of Mexico City's newest transit lines don't run under the streets or along them, but over them. The Cablebús gondolas climb out of the valley floor towards neighbourhoods built on steep hillsides, where a bus ride down to the nearest Metro station used to take the better part of an hour.

## A station like any other

The cable car is part of the city's transit network, not a tourist ride. Stations have turnstiles, each one has its own pictogram just as Metro stations do, and there is a steady stream of commuters with shopping bags and school backpacks. Cabins arrive every few seconds, slow to walking pace through the station and leave again without ever stopping.

## The view from the cabin

From above, the city reads differently. Flat roofs become the main façade: water tanks, laundry lines, satellite dishes and half-finished upper floors with steel bars left out for the next storey. Along parts of the network, roofs have been painted with large murals meant to be seen from the cabins, so the ride passes over a patchwork of colour that can't be seen from the street at all.

## Why it works here

Cable cars suit places where the terrain is the obstacle. They need a tower only every few hundred metres, so a line can cross ravines and dense blocks without much demolition, and it keeps moving in traffic that would stop a bus. Medellín showed the idea in Latin America first; Mexico City has taken it to a much larger scale.

**Tip:** ride in the late afternoon, when the light is low and the haze over the valley thins a little, and sit facing downhill.
